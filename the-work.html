Long Tide · Craniosacral Therapy

Medicine that begins by noticing.

Around the turn of the twentieth century, a final-year osteopathy student named William Garner Sutherland was examining a set of disarticulated human skull bones in his college laboratory. Fitting two of them together, he noticed the seams were bevelled and overlapping, and had the thought that they were shaped like the gills of a fish: formed for some kind of respiratory motion. His teachers found the idea eccentric. He spent the next several decades testing it.

What Sutherland observed

Sutherland’s central proposition was not that the skull bones move in themselves, but that the whole system expresses a slow, rhythmic breathing — an inhalation and an exhalation that continues whether or not you are attending to it.

He called the organising principle behind that motion the Breath of Life, a term borrowed to describe the intentions and actions of a creative intelligence at work in the body. Whatever your metaphysics, the clinical observation underneath it is humbler: the body has a rhythm you can feel with your hands, and it changes when the system is unwell.

He hit upon the idea that the sutures of the bones he was holding were like the gills of a fish and designed for some kind of respiratory motion. Michael Kern — Wisdom in the Body

Three rhythms

Practitioners describe three nested rhythms, each slower and more subtle than the last. They are not separate systems; they are described as enfolded in one another — rhythms within rhythms.

  • The cranial rhythmic impulse — the most superficial, felt at roughly eight to twelve cycles a minute.
  • The mid-tide — slower, around two and a half cycles a minute, carrying the available potency.
  • The long tide — the most formative, at roughly one hundred seconds per cycle.

Two lineages

Biomechanical work, most closely associated with John Upledger, tends to be more structural: assessing and gently encouraging movement, working with specific techniques toward a release.

Biodynamic work, associated with Franklyn Sills, is more receptive. Rather than correcting a lesion, it orientates to health — settling into stillness and following the slower tides.

My own practice is primarily biodynamic, with biomechanical training to draw on where it helps.

A note on evidence. Craniosacral therapy is widely practised but remains lightly researched. Some small studies suggest benefit for pain, migraine and infant comfort; the evidence base is not strong. What is well established is the value of calm, safe, unhurried touch. I describe the work honestly and do not sell certainty.

What it is not

  • Not chiropractic, and not a manipulation — nothing is forced or cracked.
  • Not massage: no oils, no deep tissue work, and you stay fully clothed.
  • Not a cure, and not a diagnostic service.
  • Not a replacement for your doctor, your physiotherapist, or your medication.
