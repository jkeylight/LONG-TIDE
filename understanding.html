Understanding the work

The ideas behind craniosacral therapy, in plain language. Six short sections — about three minutes, no training required.

01

The whole in every part

Cut a hologram in half and you do not get two half-pictures. You get two whole pictures, each a little softer.

The body behaves a little like this. The same pattern of health is present everywhere in it — not stored in one place, but written through all of it. That is why work at the base of the skull can change how someone stands, and why a sore shoulder is sometimes not about the shoulder at all.

A way of looking at the body, not a claim about how it works — nobody has shown that the body is a hologram.

02

One continuous fabric

The membrane that wraps your brain does not stop at the base of your skull.

It continues down as a tube around your spinal cord and anchors at your sacrum. Two firm attachments, one continuous sheet. Head and pelvis are not two things joined by a neck — mechanically, they are one piece of cloth with a head on top of it.

Practitioners work at either end. What happens at one end is felt at the other.

03

Two ways of being stuck

A fulcrum is simply the point a system organises itself around.

In a healthy body it moves. You shift your weight and the balance point shifts with you; the system keeps finding a new centre.

After an injury or a long strain, a second kind appears: a point that does not move. The body organises itself around it and stops shifting. Physically it feels like a knot. The system has built itself around a place that hurts, in order to protect it.

Most of what a practitioner is feeling for is this — not the sore place itself, but the point the body has decided to organise around.

04

When everything fires at once

Trauma is not only a memory. It is usually several things welded together:

Normally these stay somewhat separate and can be felt one at a time. After trauma they fuse, and any one of them sets off all the others at once. Someone smells something ordinary and their heart is pounding before they can think.

The work is not to break the chain, but to loosen it — so the parts can be felt separately again.

05

Why we do not go into the middle

It is tempting to think the therapy should head straight for the worst of it. It usually should not. Going into the middle of a trauma can simply re-traumatise someone.

Instead the practitioner keeps moving between two places: the edge of what is difficult, and the edge of what is fine. Touch a little of the difficulty, come back to somewhere safe, touch again. A figure of eight, back and forth.

Each pass widens the safe side a little and narrows the difficult one. The person stays present throughout, which is the whole point.

06

What actually happens in a session

Stripped of the theory, three things:

  1. 01
    Listen. The practitioner settles and does nothing in particular — no agenda, no technique being applied. The body is allowed to show what it is doing.
  2. 02
    Wait for balance. The forces in the tissue are held evenly, neither winning. There is a moment where the system stops fighting itself. It is quiet and easy to miss.
  3. 03
    Then leave it alone. Once that balance is reached, the body does the reorganising. The practitioner’s job is not to interrupt it.

You may feel warmth, or a slow unwinding, or nothing much at all. The last is common and is not a failure. The body is not a machine whose buttons can be pressed. It is a living system being given an hour of not being interfered with.

That is the whole of the theory.

The practice is mostly learning to be quiet enough to notice.
